Start here

Before anything else: most people lose money on prediction markets, and this page is not encouraging anyone to bet. Check that Polymarket is available where you live and that you meet its age requirement. Never use money you can't afford to lose.

A note on platforms: this guide is written with Polymarket in mind, but most prediction markets run on the same basic idea. Prices sit between $0 and $1 and reflect the odds, and shares pay out $1 or $0 when the question resolves. Details like fees, rules, who is allowed to use them, and how disputes get settled differ from platform to platform, so read the rules of whichever one you use.

How a market works

A market asks a yes-or-no question, like "Will Democrats win control of the House?" Each share of Yes costs between 0 and $1 and pays $1 if the answer is Yes, and nothing if it's No. A price of 62¢ means the market thinks Yes is about 62% likely. Buying Yes at 62¢ risks 62¢ to win 38¢. Buying No at 38¢ is the mirror image.

A Polymarket pick vs. owning a stock

On a screen they can look alike. Both have a price that moves, and you can buy and sell both. But they're really different things.

When you own a stock, you own a tiny slice of a real company. Its value follows how the business does: sales, profits, new products, the wider economy. There's no finish line. You can hold it for a week or for twenty years, and it never "resolves." That's why buying stocks is usually called investing. You're backing a company's ability to grow over time, though nothing is guaranteed and prices can fall as well as rise.

A Polymarket pick is a bet on how likely something is to happen. You don't own anything. You're saying "I think this outcome is more (or less) likely than the crowd does." And there's a deadline. Until then, the price of a share moves between 0¢ and $1 as people's views change, and you can sell any time at the current price without waiting for the result. But the price is always being pulled toward the end: when the question resolves, your share becomes worth exactly $1 if you were right or $0 if you were wrong. Nothing grows in the background while you wait, and one side's win is the other side's loss.

Prediction markets are short, all-or-nothing questions about what will happen, which is why the Practice page keeps the stakes pretend. Mistakes worth avoiding

  • Buying a long shot because the payout looks big. Cheap usually means unlikely.
  • Putting a lot into one race, or several races that move together. If the national mood shifts, they all lose at once.
  • Trading thin markets. Check the "thin" tag and the spread first.
  • Chasing a big move. Prices that jump on little volume often snap back.
  • Not reading the resolution rules. The exact wording decides who gets paid, so read it on the market page.
